Moral Hazard in Delegated Altruism: A Dictator Game With an Intermediary

共同研究者

  • 金栄禄（兵庫県立大学）

要約

When an agent’s actions are neither observable nor verifiable, the agent maypursue private benefits at the expense of others. Existing laboratory evidence on this moral hazard problem focuses on relationships where these actions affect the principal. We study delegated altruism, in which actions impact an uninvolved third party. A key application is charitable giving, where donors cannot easily verify how organizations use entrusted funds. In a laboratory experiment, we place an intermediary (the deliverer) in a dictator game. The dictator sends funds to the deliverer, who chooses an allocation ratio that determines the receiver’s payoff. Since the dictator’s own payoff does not depend on this choice, the deliverer’s action is hidden. Compared to a setting without the hidden action problem—where the dictator specifies the allocation ratio—the hidden action environment reduces the average amount sent by 4.31 tokens (35%) and the receiver’s average payoff by 12.98 tokens (85%). The larger decline in the receiver’s payoff arises because deliverers also choose a lower allocation ratio. The deliverer’s non-binding promise, announced before the dictator decides, restores giving almost to the level observed when there is no hidden action problem, but deliverers do not follow through on the promise. Consequently, the allocation ratio remains as low as in the hidden action environment. Receiver payoffs, therefore, improve modestly. We term this phenomenon the promise illusion: communication can recover donations without sufficiently recovering beneficiary welfare.
